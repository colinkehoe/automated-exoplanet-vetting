Automated exoplanet vetting

Most TESS transit candidates are not planets. This model reads the light curve and the host star, scores each candidate, and shows the evidence behind every score.

From a candidate to a score

1 · FetchDownload every TESS sector for the star, stitch them, and remove slow brightness drifts without touching the transits.
2 · MeasureFold the light curve on the candidate's period and run the diagnostics below — 30 numbers per candidate.
3 · ScoreA gradient-boosted classifier trained on TOIs that already have a verdict, with probabilities calibrated so they can be read as odds.
4 · ExplainRe-score each candidate with one feature swapped for a typical value; the change in log-odds is that feature's contribution.

What the diagnostics look for

Odd/even depth

Two stars eclipsing each other can be mistaken for one planet at half the true period. Then alternate dips have different depths.

Secondary eclipse

A second, shallower dip half an orbit later means the companion is bright enough to be a star, not a planet.

Transit shape

A planet crossing a star gives a flat-bottomed dip; a grazing pair of stars gives a V.

Centroid shift

If the centre of light moves during the dip, the star that dimmed is a neighbour, not the target.

Host-star fit

A planet's transit duration follows from the star's density and the orbital period. Durations that don't fit point to an eclipsing binary or a blend.

Per-transit consistency

Real transits repeat. A signal carried by one or two events is usually instrumental.

How well it does

Cross-validation keeps every TOI of the same star in one fold, so sibling candidates can't leak across the split. The harder test trains only on candidates alerted before 2021 and scores the later ones — the situation the model is actually in.

An independent check

Ten scored candidates had already been called by the TESS team, using a label the model never saw. Confirmed planets land near the top, eclipsing binaries near the bottom.

Read this before believing it

Caveats

  • This is a ranking, not a discovery. A high score means "worth observing next", not "this is a planet". Confirming one needs follow-up observations this project does not do.
  • The probabilities are optimistic in absolute terms. They are calibrated against a training set that is 55% planets. Unresolved candidates are likely a good deal less planet-rich, so treat the ordering as the real output.
  • Expect ~0.90, not ~0.95, on new candidates. Recent TOIs are fainter and noisier than the ones the model learned from.
  • The two pipelines are scored by different models, and their numbers are not comparable. SPOC 2-minute light curves resolve the shape of a transit; QLP full-frame images sample every 30 minutes and blur it. On the same 2,422 TOIs the SPOC model reaches ROC-AUC 0.957 against QLP's 0.929, so rank candidates within a pipeline, not across the two.
  • The catalogue ephemeris is taken on trust. Periods and epochs are not re-fitted, so a wrong period means the diagnostics measure the wrong thing.
  • Coverage still is not everything. A candidate needs a light curve from one of the two pipelines; TOIs with neither, or with no usable ephemeris in the catalogue, are absent.
  • Part of a high score is the host star. Nearby small stars host more confirmed planets, and the model has learned that prior along with the transit evidence.
